# Puzzle Reel

A swipe feed of seven games with a research session harness: four solver-scored puzzles, a speed-of-processing task, a dual task and an object-location memory task.

- **Outbound**: a parking lot. Cars only drive forward; a tapped car leaves the lot or rolls until it bumps something. From level 3, lots contain pinwheels (four cars that each block the next, so one must first slide part of the way); level 4 adds a planted trap (a move after which the lot can no longer be cleared). With feedback on, levels 1 to 3 mark every move and flag a dead end at once; levels 4 and 5 stay quiet until no car can move.
- **Conduit**: a garden. Turn pipe tiles until water from the well reaches every plant with no leaks. Every pipe stops at a collar just inside its tile, and where two pipe ends meet across the gap between tiles a coupling joins them, so a connection is never a guess: joined ends are coupled, and an end that meets nothing is plainly open. The water visibly runs through each connected pipe from the well, so every flower that opens shows which turn watered it. Every board has exactly one solution, and every pipe is part of it: a board where the plants could be watered around some pipe is never dealt, so a garden in full bloom is always a solved board.
- **Tumbler**: a vault. Crack a hidden color code. Clues either mark every pin (right spot, elsewhere, absent, as in Wordle) or give only the two counts, as in classic Mastermind.
- **Glimpse**: speed of processing in the useful-field-of-view format. An animal in the middle and, from level 2, a bird around it flash briefly (1.5 s at first, shorter as you get it right), then a pattern mask. Each question is drawn where it is asked: a "?" where the animal was, above the two animals to pick from; then a "?" in the middle and the outline of the thing to find in each spot round it, while a light runs round them. After the flash a crowd of shapes (the mask) holds for 0.6 s and then stays faintly behind the questions. In the first practice flash, rings mark the two things to remember. With feedback on (free play, practice), a wrong animal ends the look at once; research sessions always ask both. Display time adapts on every look.
- **Stacks**: the tower-of-London task. Pieces on three posts; lift the top piece of a post and set it on another until the arrangement matches the goal, in as few moves as possible. Planning time before the first move is logged. Every level is a place of its own, with its own pieces and posts, and two levels add a rule (the same in free play and research sessions; practice uses the stones): river stones on stakes (1), suitcases on luggage trolleys at the station (2), containers at the harbor (3), a cake of four layers of different sizes on three stands, where a layer only goes on a bigger one (4, 7 moves), and scoops on three ice cream cones that hold three each, where nothing can go on the scoop with the cherry (5, 7 moves). The cake comes before the ice cream because its rule leaves fewer wrong moves: played through by the computer, every level is passed less often than the one before. A move the rule refuses leaves the piece up, shakes the piece in the way and says the rule ("Small goes on big", "The cherry stays on top"), which is also said once at the start of each card at those levels.
- **Switchback**: the dual-task format (adaptive multitasking training). Steer a car along a winding road and tap the target sign whenever it comes up (the sign itself, or the button that shows it); let every other sign pass. Each place has its own animal on the target sign, and now and then that animal wanders onto the road: tap it and it moves on. Things to collect lie along the road. How long a sign stays up and how fast the car goes both adapt as you drive.
- **Keepsake**: object-location memory. A few things sit on stepping stones across a park; after a short look they hop into a basket, and you put each back on its stone. Levels add things and stones.

Each game opens with a short practice round and a guiding hand, so it can be learned by playing. A practice round has to be played right before the game goes on (a puzzle solved; Glimpse with at most one miss, Switchback with every sign right, Keepsake with every thing on its stone). If it is not, it says "Once more" and comes round again, up to three tries, and only then moves on; practice rows log `success` as that pass. The cards after practice start at level 1. Rounds of the three trial games (Glimpse, Switchback, Keepsake) get confetti only at three stars or a passed practice; two stars get the win chime without confetti, and fewer a soft note. Puzzle moves are scored against a solver: distance to the goal before and after each move for Outbound and Conduit, and expected versus best-possible information (bits) for each Tumbler try.

## Levels and effort

Every game has levels 1 to 5, and a level asks about the same effort in each game. The fewest taps that finish a card, averaged over generated cards (`npm test` prints and checks this table):

| 2 | 7 (6×6, 7 cars) | 7.6 (4×4) | 9.3 (3 pins, 4 colors) | 9.1 (2 pins, 5 colors) | 9 (4 looks) | 8 (4 moves) | 9 (7 of 12, 1) | 9 (4 of 7) |
| 3 | 9 (6×6, 8 cars, 1 pinwheel) | 9.6 (4×5) | 10.7 (3 pins, 5 colors) | 11.2 (3 pins, 3 colors) | 11 (5 looks) | 10 (5 moves) | 12 (9 of 16, 2) | 11 (5 of 8) |
| 4 | 11 (7×7, 10 cars, 1 pinwheel, 1 trap) | 11.7 (5×5) | 13.3 (4 pins, 5 colors) | 13.7 (3 pins, 4 colors) | 13 (6 looks) | 12 (6 moves) | 14 (11 of 20, 2) | 13 (6 of 10) |
| 5 | 13 (7×7, 11 cars, 2 pinwheels) | 14.5 (5×6) | 13.7 (4 pins, 6 colors) | 14.0 (3 pins, 5 colors) | 13 (6 looks) | 14 (7 moves) | 16 (13 of 24, 2) | 15 (7 of 12) |

Outbound counts the solver's moves, Conduit the turns needed, Tumbler the taps along the most informative tries (pins already in the right spot carry into the next row with per-pin clues), Glimpse one tap to start plus one per answer, Stacks two taps per move (lift, set down), Switchback one tap to start plus one per target sign and one per animal on the road (steering and collecting are not counted), and Keepsake one tap to look plus two per thing (pick up, put down). Thinking time is not in these numbers; compare `duration_ms` by game and level in pilot data.

Difficulty grows inside each game in its own way: Outbound needs slides from level 3 and avoids a trap at level 4; Conduit levels 4 and 5 keep the board that needs the most search out of 8; Tumbler adds pins and colors; Glimpse widens the field and adds distractors; Stacks needs more moves, which means more pieces parked out of the way first; Switchback brings tighter, more frequent bends on a narrower road, other signs that look more and more like the target, and more animals on the road; Keepsake puts out more things and more empty stones, with a little more time to look. Levels move the same way in every game. In research blocks, two successes in a row move a level up and one miss moves it down (2-up/1-down), where a success is a solve within par (1.5 times the solver's moves or tries) or, for Glimpse, a round at or under the level's display-time target (Switchback: a drive at or under the level's sign time and at or over its speed). Free play uses the same rule with a quick start: one level up per success until the first miss in that game. In free play the run of successes toward the next level carries over between visits. A skipped or abandoned card never counts as a miss. Skip goes straight to the next card: one tap in free play; in research sessions (when allowed) a second tap on the same button, and the skipped card still uses up its place in the block. Switchback never moves down a level, because its timing already adapts as you play; neither does Glimpse in research blocks, but in free play a Glimpse round with fewer than half the looks right steps back a level, since at its slowest flash the level is too much for now.

## Tumbler

Tumbler has to be obvious to someone who has never played a game, is anxious or forgetful: with feedback on (free play and its practice) the board remembers and explains, and stops mistakes before they cost a try.

- **The lock.** Above the rows, one socket per hidden pin with a question mark; a pin found in its place lands in its socket and stays there. The first time (and in practice) the lock says "Find the 2 hidden colors".
- **Words with the colors.** After a try, the clues are said in words under their tiles, one after the other: "Right color, right place", "Right color, wrong place", "Wrong color" (no word the player cannot see, such as "code" or "pin") (in practice always, in free play until six codes are cracked).
- **The colors remember.** A color found gets a green ring, one in the code but not yet placed a gold ring, one not hidden at all fades back (its own color, faint and a little smaller), in the rows too, and cannot be picked ("Wrong color"); once as many colors are known to be hidden as there are places, every other color fades back as well.
- **Not here, but there.** A color put where it is known not to be ("Not here") moves on to the next place where it can go; if there is none, it is not taken. Found pins are already filled in. The practice hand points at a color that is in the code, then at Check (the button that checks the row; it said Try until app 2.22), which pulses once the row is full.
- **No wasted tries.** A try identical to an earlier one is not taken (`repeat_blocked`); the earlier row lights up.

With feedback off (research blocks) none of this applies, so tries are the player's own.

Tumbler's per-pin clues are meant to be read at a glance, by older players too: the clue is the tile, as in Wordle: a pin in the right place sits on a green tile, a hidden color in another place on a gold one (the pins of the latest try wiggle), and a color that is not hidden fades back, keeping its color; balls on tiles get a white rim so green on green still shows. No corner marks, no meters on the rows, and the tries still to come are slim bars, so the board fits on a phone. With feedback on, a try identical to an earlier one is not taken: the row shakes and the earlier row lights up (`repeat_blocked` in the log), since playing it again says nothing new.

## Glimpse

| Level | Shows | Like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | the middle item (for example rabbit or hedgehog) and a target on the inner ring, 8 directions | divided attention, close in |
| 2 | the target on the middle ring, as in practice | divided attention |
| 3 | the target on any of 3 rings | wider field |
| 4 | plus 8 distractors (shells at sea) | selective attention |
| 5 | 16 distractors (clouds in the sky) | selective attention |

Until app 2.20, level 1 showed the middle item only (processing speed). Practice and every level now ask the same two things, so what is practised is what is played. Until app 2.21, level 4 had 23 distractors and level 5 had 47 with a harder pair (such as cat or fox): a cliff for older players, now taken in steps of 8.

- **Timing.** Everything shown during a look is drawn on one canvas inside `requestAnimationFrame`, so the flash and the mask change on screen frames. The frame period is measured during each fixation (and snapped to a standard rate within 1.5%), display times are whole frames, and each look logs the planned and the measured on-screen time plus a `frame_drop` flag. Frame timestamps are not photometry: check a new device type with a photodiode before relying on very short times.
- **Rounds.** 4 looks at levels 1 and 2, 5 at level 3, 6 at levels 4 and 5, so rounds grow with the level like the puzzles do. The first look starts on a tap; the rest follow by themselves. Each look opens with a fixation ring that closes slowly over about a second (fixation 1200 to 1499 ms), then the flash, a 600 ms mask (a crowd of shapes; it stops the picture lingering, so the flash time is the real viewing time, and what matters for that is that it comes at once, not how long it stays), which then fades but stays faintly behind the questions, then the answers and short feedback: about 15 to 25 seconds a round.
- **Staircase.** Display times come from a ladder from 2 s down by x0.9 per step, rounded to frames (at 60 Hz: 120 … 1 frames). One step shorter after a correct look, six steps longer after a miss (weighted up/down, settles at about 86% correct, so most looks are hits). Display times never go below 50 ms: a player who gets there moves up a level instead of chasing ever shorter flashes. A fresh staircase starts at 1.5 s, a slow and easy look for older players and non-gamers (the practice ends at 0.7 s), and earns every step. It carries over between rounds of the same level (within a block in research sessions, between visits in free play), and each carried-over round starts two steps slower so it opens with a likely hit. A new level starts three steps slower than the level below ended, because it asks more.
- **Levels.** Every level asks for the animal and the thing around it, as practice does; level 1 keeps the thing close to the middle. A round counts once a right answer brings the flash, as the counter shows it, to the level's target or under; two such rounds in a row move up a level (one, until the first round that does not count). In free play a level opens slow the first time, three steps above its target (about 1.3 s, and 1.6 s at levels 4 and 5), so three right answers in a row reach the target and a new level never starts at the speed the last one ended; a round with fewer than half the looks right steps back a level, and that level opens slow again next time. The result shows how far the flash got, and after a round that did not count, the target for the next level. The target is a one-second look at levels 1 to 3 and 1.2 s at levels 4 and 5, where things get in the way: human speeds for older players and non-gamers, so the levels add more to see rather than asking for a faster eye. These are game settings, not study values. The flash keeps adapting inside every level, so quick players still see it get shorter.
- **Field size.** The field scales with the screen (radius logged as `field_px`). On a phone held at 35 cm the outer ring sits about 4 to 5 degrees from the middle, on a 24-inch monitor at 60 cm about 6. The clinical test places its targets about 11 cm out, which is 10 degrees or more at desktop viewing distances, so eccentricity is the main setting gap on phones.
- **Scenes.** The scene follows the level: a meadow at levels 1 and 2 (rabbit or hedgehog; find a bird, butterfly or snail among leaves), the sea at 3 and 4 (fish or turtle; a starfish, sailboat or jellyfish among shells) and a sunset sky at 5 (plane or balloon; a kite, rocket or star among clouds), with the target changing from look to look. Practice uses the meadow with the bird. The scene is logged with each round and the target with each look.
- **Stimuli** are our own silhouettes, not the vehicles of the clinical test, so a check built on that test stays a transfer measure.

## Switchback

- **Driving.** The car keeps moving sideways while the wheel is turned, and a bend pushes it outward, more the faster it goes, so holding a bend takes steady steering. Drag anywhere on the road to steer (or use the arrow keys); the wheel shows the lock. Time on the road is logged; leaving it slows the car.
- **Signs.** One sign at a time comes up just right of the road ahead. Tap it when it is the target: on the sign itself (test players went for the sign, not the button), on the sign button at the bottom right, which shows the target, or with Space. The target is the place's animal on a warning triangle. Other signs grow more similar by level: blue signs, red-and-white round signs, warning triangles with symbols, triangles with other animals (bird, snail, turtle), then look-alikes (geese, swans and hens in the village; reindeer, deer and bears in the north). The target stays the same for the whole drive. Next to the speed at the top, the target sign stands with one dot per target sign in the drive: a dot turns green when that sign is caught and red when it is missed (with feedback off it just fills in), so what counts is in view the whole time.
- **Animals on the road.** Once a drive at L1 and L2 and twice from L3, the place's animal steps onto the road ahead between two signs and stands in the middle. The car eases off while it is there; a tap on it (or H) and the horn toots, it hurries off, and the car picks up speed again. If it is still there when the car gets close, the car brakes and the animal moves on by itself. In the village the duck brings her ducklings. These moments have their own 3 s between signs, so they never overlap a sign, and the speed measure leaves out the time from the animal stepping out until the car is back to speed (`brake_s`).
- **Things to collect.** Short rows of things float over the road, one kind for each place (apples, watermelon, carrots, water drops, acorns, bananas, starfish, opals, cherries, snowflakes). Driving through them picks them up; they are counted in the corner and logged (`n_items`, `n_collected`), but they are not tapped and do not enter any task measure.
- **Places.** Ten places, two for each level and time of day; the seed picks one. L1 morning: a farm with hedgerows (cows) or the savanna under a snowy mountain, with a balloon over the acacias (elephants). L2 noon: wheat and lavender fields (rabbits) or the desert dunes with a caravan far off (camels). L3 afternoon: a pine forest under snowy peaks (deer) or the jungle, with mist and parrots (monkeys). L4 sunset: the coast road beside the sea (crabs) or the red outback (kangaroos). L5: a village street at dusk (ducks) or the north at night under the northern lights (moose). The landmark at the end (barn, baobab, windmill, oasis, cabin, waterfall, lighthouse, great red rock, village, red cottage) sits small on the skyline where the road runs out; in the last seconds of the drive it comes into reach as a real place beside the road, the car drives through the chequered finish line in front of it and rolls to a stop just past it. The road, its lines, the sign shapes and the car are the same everywhere; `place` is logged, so the animal and the scenery are a covariate within a level. Practice is on the farm, with the cow.
- **Feedback.** With feedback on, a caught target's animal comes off the sign and runs or hops into the field (about 1 s, after the tap); a missed target or a tap on another sign gets a red ring. With feedback off, signs just go. A soft engine hum follows the speed when sound is on.
- **Adapting.** A sign stays up 1.5 s in a fresh round; a caught target makes the next one shorter, a missed target or a tap on another sign longer (settles near 80% of targets caught). Every 1.5 s the speed goes up while the car stays on the road and down when it leaves. Both carry over to the next drive at the same level. A drive succeeds when a caught target brings the sign time to the level's or under and the average speed meets the level's; a drive that does not shows the two targets on its result, the one it met ticked.
- **Evidence.** The format of the Focus core in the concept paper (Anguera et al. 2013: 12 hours over four weeks at home, ages 60 to 85, effects after six months). The animals on the road and the things to collect are our additions: an occasional second response and a reason to steer. They sit in their own time between signs, but they make a drive longer and busier than the original, so compare with the published effect with that in mind. As with every core, the format is proven, not our dose.

## Keepsake

- **The map.** One park, always the same, drawn by hand: a house, a pond, a big tree, a well, a flower bed and a bench along a winding path, with twelve stones placed beside them. The landmarks stay put so they can be learned; each card brings out 4 to 8 stones and puts 2 to 6 things on them, from the seed. Stones and things are drawn large enough to read and tap at arm's length.
- **A card.** Tap the eye to look: the things appear and a timer runs on the basket (5 to 11 s by level; a tap on the basket ends the look early and is logged). The things hop into the basket, then you drag or tap each back onto a stone. One try per thing.
- **Feedback.** With feedback on (free play), a thing on its own stone turns green; one on a wrong stone turns red and moves to its own stone. With feedback off, things stay where they are put.
- **Scoring.** Each placement logs the right and the chosen stone, the distance between them and the time it took. A card succeeds with at most one thing on a wrong stone. Evidence level: near (object-location memory is an established measurement paradigm).

## Profile

The menu's Profile has two views in one sheet.

- **Every game at a glance**, on one phone screen: three big numbers (stars, days played, minutes), then the seven games under the concept paper's five areas (Reaction, Memory, Patterns, Problem solving, Focus). Each row shows the game's picture, its level ladder from Beginner to Master with the next step filling up (half filled when one of the two good cards for it is done; a lighter tint up to the best level reached, for games that can step back), the level's name and the game's stars.
- **One game's own page**, opened by tapping its row (the arrow at the top goes back to the overview, where it was):
  - a header card with the game's picture, its area, level and ladder;
  - **Next**: the next level's name and what it brings (Outbound: cars and lot; Conduit: garden size; Tumbler: pins and colors; Stacks: moves and the place, with the rule at 4 and 5; Switchback: places and signs; Keepsake: things; Glimpse: where the second thing can be), the good cards still needed as slots that fill in, what a good card is in the game's own terms (Glimpse: a flash at or under the level's target; Switchback: sign time and speed; the others: two or three stars), and a Play button straight into that game;
  - **the player's own curve**: the last twelve rounds, newest on the right. Glimpse shows each round's flash and Switchback each drive's sign time and speed as bars, with a notch at the target that round's level had; bars that met it are in the game's color. The puzzles show each card's stars, Keepsake the things put back on the right stone. Tapping a round reads it out (moves against the fewest, sign time, things right). When the first two and the last two rounds at the current level show a clear gain, a small badge with a rising arrow sits on that chart (Glimpse: ms shorter; Switchback: sign time shorter, speed higher); otherwise nothing is shown;
  - **three numbers**: cards (rounds, drives), minutes, and one figure of the game's own (fewest-move solves, tries per code, planning time, fastest correct look, best speed, cards with every thing right);
  - **all five levels**, Beginner to Master, each with what it brings: the ones passed checked, the current one highlighted.

The profile speaks only in the games' own terms: no evidence levels, studies or explanations on screen (those live in this README and the codebook).

In free play the good cards toward the next level carry over between visits, so a run is never lost by closing the app. After a good card that does not level up yet, the result says "One more for Expert" with a slot that fills in; a level up names the new level and what it brings ("Level up · Pro" over "5 moves · the harbor"), and a new record is marked in gold (see Progress and records). A puzzle's result reads its moves against the fewest possible ("4 moves · fewest 4"); Tumbler's says "aim", because its reference is a good guesser's tries and luck can beat it. Switchback's result puts what counts first (signs caught, time on the road) and the things collected on a smaller line below.

Everything comes from free play on this device (local storage: `pr1.gstats` totals, `pr1.ghist` the last 24 rounds per game, `pr1.days` the days played, `pr1.lvrun` the run toward each next level; the first three are built from the logs the first time). They are game progress only: nothing in the profile scores an area, because area values belong to the checks.

## Progress and records

Three loops, from seconds to weeks, so that playing always works toward something you can see.

- **Every card has a target and a record.** The target is what is possible: the fewest moves or turns (the solver), the computer's tries in Tumbler (its own most informative guesses), the level's flash or sign time. The record is your best at this level in the game's own measure: the fastest solve (Outbound, Conduit, Stacks), the fewest tries (Tumbler), the shortest flash reached (Glimpse), the best average speed (Switchback), the shortest look with everything right (Keepsake). It sits in the head as a trophy chip while you play: it fades and is struck through once it is out of reach (the puzzles: the clock passes it; Tumbler: the tries reach it) and turns gold when a round is on its way to a new one (Glimpse: a right answer brings the flash under it). The result says "New record" in gold with the old one struck beside it, or shows the record to beat; the first card at a level sets it. Tumbler adds "Fewer tries than the computer" when a code is cracked in fewer tries than the computer's way of guessing. The puzzles' result shows the time in the same terms as the record (24.3 s), Keepsake's the look, Switchback's the average speed. Records are kept per game and level on the device (`pr1.records`, free play only; Glimpse's earlier shortest flashes carry over).
- **Every card shows what it counts for.** When a card starts, a cue under the head shows what the next level takes and the good cards so far ("★★ ○○ for Advanced"; Glimpse and Switchback show their targets); tapping the level dots shows it again. The next level's dot glows gold while this card can still count (the puzzles: while two or three stars are still possible).
- **Every new level is a moment.** The first time a game reaches a level, the screen turns to it: the game's badge takes the level's medal (bronze for Advanced, silver for Pro, gold for Expert, gold with a crown for Master), the ladder fills a step, a burst of the game's colors, a fanfare and a buzz, and what the level brings ("5 moves · the harbor"); "Play Pro" goes straight on, "See my result" goes back to the card. Coming back to a level after stepping down gets the result's pill only (`pr1.reached` keeps the highest level each game has reached). The medals stay on the game's badge in the menu, the title row and the profile, so the menu is also a shelf of what you have reached.
- **Every level is a place.** Each level of a game has a look of its own, with the same rules and pieces: the colors, the board's materials and a scene drawn around the board change, so moving up is also arriving somewhere new, and the level up and the profile's level list name the place. Outbound: the town lot, the beach, the city at night, the ski lodge, the ferry at sunset. Conduit: the herb garden, the vegetable patch, the greenhouse, the desert oasis, the palace garden at night (each with its own flowers). Tumbler: the bike lock, the school locker, the front door, the safe, the vault. Keepsake: the same park in spring, summer, autumn, winter and at night, so its landmarks can still be learned. Glimpse: the meadow, the meadow at dusk, the sea, the sea at night, the sunset sky. Stacks goes further: each level has its own pieces and posts too, and levels 4 and 5 their own rule (see Stacks above): the river stones, the station, the harbor, the bakery, the ice cream parlor. Switchback already drives through two places a level, morning to night. Practice keeps the first look. Research sessions play the same levels as free play, looks and all.
- **Touch.** Phones that vibrate buzz on these moments; on iPhone, where Safari has no vibration, they tap the Taptic Engine through a switch control (Safari 17.4 and later; it may only fire close to a tap).

## Build and test

No dependencies; Node 18 or later.

```
npm run build   # writes index.html and dist/puzzle-reel.html (standalone, the same page) and dist/artifact.html (claude.ai)
npm test        # determinism, solvability, labels vs brute force, both clue styles, every Conduit pipe needed, taps per level, Glimpse balance and staircase vs a simulated observer, Switchback places, signs, animals, things and staircases, Keepsake placement, speed
```

Puzzle generation runs in a Web Worker built from the same engine source, with a main-thread fallback.

## Hosting

`dist/puzzle-reel.html` is one self-contained HTML file, so any static host works, including GitHub Pages (deploy from the `main` branch; the build also writes the page to `index.html`, so the site root serves the app itself with no redirect). Outside claude.ai the Save buttons download files directly.

## Reproducibility

A puzzle's seed is `fnv1a(sessionSeed | block | game | level | occurrence)`, and generators are deterministic, so the same session seed and plan give the same puzzles on every device. For Glimpse the seed fixes what is shown in every look (animal, bird position, leaves, fixation time, mask); display times follow the participant's answers. Practice rounds are fixed. The puzzle id (`B{block}-{game}-L{level}-{n}`, or `P-{game}` for practice) and seed are logged with every event. Regenerate any puzzle with `Engines.games[game].generate(level, seed, clueStyle)`.

## Data

Logs stay in the browser's local storage until exported from the Data panel as CSV (moves and looks, puzzles and rounds) or JSON. The in-app codebook ("What the columns mean", or Save codebook) documents every column and move label, the Glimpse levels and its timing method.
